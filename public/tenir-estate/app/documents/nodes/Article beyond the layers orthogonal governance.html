1. The starting point: controlling systems that can act

Autonomous AI systems do more than produce answers. They can call tools, send requests, change records, move money, delegate work, and trigger events in the outside world. Once AI can act, safety becomes a question of controlling execution—not just improving the answer it gives.

Imagine a business using several AI agents. One handles invoices, another manages purchasing, and a third coordinates operations. Each has permission to act within a set of limits.

Even if every agent follows its own rules, the business can still encounter problems. Two agents might spend from the same budget. A request might be sent twice after a timeout. An approval might be applied to a different operation than the one originally reviewed. Or the system might believe a transaction failed when it actually succeeded.

These problems share an important feature: they cannot always be solved by improving the agent's reasoning. They also require reliable execution, clear authority, accurate evidence, and rules that apply across interacting parts of the system.

The question that connects the whole discussion What must remain true as an autonomous system acts—and how can the system enforce and verify those conditions when information is incomplete?

2. Governance and middleware: two different jobs

Governance and middleware are closely related, but they answer different questions. Confusing them can leave a system with good rules but weak enforcement—or strong enforcement of rules that are too narrow.

1

Governance defines what is allowed

Who may act? Which tools can they use? What limits apply? Which actions need approval? What conditions must remain true?

2

Middleware and runtime enforce constraints

Check requests, prevent duplicate execution, manage concurrency, enforce limits, and block or pause unsafe transitions.

3

Evidence helps establish what happened

Receipts, logs, external status checks, and reconciled records help distinguish success, failure, and unresolved outcomes.

4

System-wide checks connect the pieces

Check whether several individually valid actions together violate a budget, safety limit, or broader system commitment.

For example, governance might say that an agent may spend no more than $1,000. Middleware can stop the same payment request from being dispatched twice. A ledger can help establish how much was actually spent.

The rule, the enforcement mechanism, and the evidence are three different things. A reliable system needs all three.

3. Why thinking only in software layers is not enough

Architecture diagrams commonly show a stack: agent, policy engine, middleware, runtime, external service. Such diagrams explain where components live and how information flows between them.

But the location of a rule does not tell us everything about what that rule governs. A policy service might evaluate an agent's authority, a particular payment, a sequence of earlier actions, or the balance of a shared account.

Consider two authorized agents making different payments. A middleware component may prevent duplicate requests perfectly, yet the total spending can still exceed the shared budget. The problem is not a broken duplicate check. It is that the policy was too narrow.

The essential distinction A component can enforce its local rule correctly while the overall system still reaches an unsafe result. Local correctness and global safety are not the same thing.

4. What exactly is being governed?

A more complete view begins by identifying the objects that policies apply to. The categories below are a practical framework for organizing the problem—not a universal standard or a claim that every system needs a separate service for each category.

1

Agent or principal

Identity, authority, permissions, tools, and spending limits. May this entity act?

2

Action or operation

A particular payment, message, file change, or tool call. Is this request permitted?

3

Transition or passage

A specific change across an execution boundary. Is this the exact change that was approved?

4

Trajectory or workflow

A sequence of actions and outcomes. Does the course of execution remain acceptable?

5

Relationship or delegation

Authority passed between agents, dependencies, and trust boundaries. Who may act for whom?

6

Shared resource

Budgets, accounts, inventory, capacity, and records used by several actors. Will shared limits hold?

7

Mission or system invariant

Conditions that must remain true across the whole system, regardless of which agent acts.

These objects are connected. A payment, for example, belongs to an operation, is initiated by an agent, crosses a transition, changes an account, and becomes part of a history of spending.

The same payment can therefore be checked from several angles. The agent must be authorized. The transition must match the approved request. The operation must not be retried unsafely. The account must remain within its budget. The combined sequence must respect the broader policy.

5. From objects to properties: what must remain true?

Listing objects is useful, but the real purpose of governance is to preserve properties—conditions that must hold as the system changes.

Examples include:

  • An agent must not use a tool outside its authority.
  • A permit must not be reused for a different transition.
  • An unresolved payment must not be blindly resubmitted.
  • Total spending must stay within the applicable budget.
  • Delegation must not grant more authority than the delegator possesses.
  • A system-wide safety condition must hold even when several agents act concurrently.

Each property needs a defined scope, relevant context, a point where it is checked, a mechanism that enforces it, and evidence that supports the resulting decision.

6. The execution lifecycle: from permission to evidence

Governance cannot end when a request receives approval. The system must also manage execution, observe the result, and respond to new information.

  1. Evaluate. Identify the actor, requested action, current state, applicable rules, and important uncertainties.
  2. Decide. Allow, deny, hold, or escalate the operation.
  3. Enforce. Verify the execution identity and apply runtime protections such as idempotency, single-use permits, and concurrency controls.
  4. Observe. Collect the response, receipt, status, or other relevant evidence.
  5. Reconcile. If the result is unclear, consult a reliable source instead of guessing.
  6. Reassess. Update the system's view of the operation and check whether later actions remain permissible.

This is a feedback loop, not a one-time approval stamp. A decision that was reasonable before execution may need to be reconsidered when the external result becomes known.

7. Governing uncertainty: what if the system does not know?

AI systems are often discussed in terms of uncertainty about their answers. Autonomous systems face another kind of uncertainty: uncertainty about what happened after a command was sent.

Imagine that an assistant sends a payment request. The payment service processes the payment, but the confirmation is lost. The assistant sees a timeout.

The timeout does not tell the assistant whether the payment happened. There are at least two possible realities:

Possibility A: no payment

The request did not reach the service or the service did not commit it.

Possibility B: payment completed

The service committed the payment, but the acknowledgment never arrived.

The system should represent this as UNKNOWN, not silently convert it to success or failure.

The next step may be to query the service, check a transaction ledger, use a stable idempotency key, or ask an authorized person to intervene. Only after enough evidence is available should the system decide whether a retry is safe.

Uncertainty is not one single problem

  • Missing knowledge: the system lacks facts needed for a decision.
  • Stale state: the system's records may no longer match reality.
  • Execution uncertainty: an external action's outcome is unresolved.
  • Ambiguous authority: the system does not know which instruction or policy governs.
  • Uncertain prediction: the model cannot confidently predict what will happen.

These situations need different responses. A model confidence score cannot prove that a remote transaction was committed. That requires evidence about the transaction itself.

Fail closed does not mean give up It means do not take a risky next step while a necessary condition remains unproven. The system can hold, investigate, reconcile, or escalate instead of proceeding blindly.

8. The execution membrane: a practical example

The paper Governing the Unobserved: A Fail-Closed Membrane for Cross-Realm Execution under Post-Admission Epistemic Uncertainty explores a focused version of this problem.

Its central idea is an execution membrane: a controlled boundary that tracks a logical operation, its individual attempts, its execution permissions, and the evidence about what happened.

Instead of relying on a general instruction such as “be careful with retries,” the design gives the operation a stable identity and preserves its history. It distinguishes an unresolved attempt from a confirmed failure and requires evidence to support retry decisions.

What the design helps clarify

  • Permission to execute is different from proof that execution succeeded.
  • A confirmed failure is different from an unknown outcome.
  • A retry should depend on evidence, not simply on a timeout.
  • An approval must remain bound to the specific transition it approved.
  • Local records must not be treated as proof of external reality unless the required observation assumptions hold.

What the design does not automatically prove

The membrane's safety claims depend on explicit assumptions about how external effects are produced and observed. If a service can commit a payment while the local system loses the acknowledgment and fails to persist evidence, the local system may not be able to prove that no payment occurred.

A bounded model can help reveal counterexamples and compare alternative designs, but a finite set of simulated scenarios is not a universal guarantee of production safety.

The paper's reported 53-trace comparison illustrates this distinction: target-side idempotency prevented duplicates in the modeled traces, while evidence-bound reconciliation improved outcome classification under its stated reliability assumptions. Those results are useful within the model; they are not real-world probabilities or proof of universal correctness.

The membrane is therefore best understood as one possible mechanism for enforcing transition- and operation-level safety—not as a complete governance system for every agent, resource, and mission.

9. From local safety to global safety

A system can prevent duplicate requests and still violate its overall rules. This is the difference between protecting one operation and protecting a shared resource or whole trajectory.

Suppose two agents are authorized to spend $1,000 each. They send different payments to the same account. Both payments execute exactly once, but the account has only a $1,500 budget.

To prevent this, the system needs a property that applies to the shared account and all relevant transactions—not just to each agent individually. It may need an atomic reservation or transaction mechanism so that two agents cannot both spend the same remaining funds.

The same principle applies to other domains:

  • Several agents consume more computing capacity than the system can safely provide.
  • Several workflow steps create an irreversible outcome that no single step would have caused alone.
  • An agent delegates work to another agent whose authority is insufficient.
  • A sequence of valid tool calls gradually crosses a cumulative risk threshold.

The system must therefore govern both individual objects and the relationships and shared state connecting them.

10. A joined-up architecture

An object-centric approach does not require one central service to make every decision. Different responsibilities can remain distributed across the system, provided they share a clear account of the rules and evidence that matter.

The architecture needs five connected capabilities:

  1. An object and relationship map so the system knows which actors, operations, resources, and histories are relevant.
  2. Policy and invariant evaluation to determine which properties apply in the current context.
  3. Runtime enforcement to make decisions effective at the execution boundary.
  4. Evidence and reconciliation to establish outcomes and resolve uncertainty.
  5. Trajectory and system-wide monitoring to check cumulative effects and cross-agent constraints.

The goal is not to put everything in one place. The goal is to stop individual components from making locally reasonable decisions that contradict the system's broader safety requirements.

11. What remains difficult?

The shift from layers to governed objects gives us a better way to ask the questions. It does not make the hard engineering problems disappear.

External reality is not always observable

A local log may say that no success was observed, but that is not proof that no external effect occurred. Reliable reconciliation may require target-side idempotency, an authoritative status service, transactional cooperation, or an independently verifiable receipt.

Concurrency changes the picture

Two requests may be evaluated against the same stale state and then execute concurrently. Preventing that may require atomic reservations, version checks, serialization, or other mechanisms that connect the decision to the actual state change.

Evidence needs more than a valid signature

A signature can help establish that evidence has not been altered and was signed by a particular key. It does not, on its own, prove that the key is trustworthy, that its owner had authority, that the evidence is fresh, or that it refers to the correct transition.

Safety and progress must be balanced

A system that blocks every uncertain operation forever may avoid some unsafe retries but become unusable. A robust design needs clear rules for holds, escalation, recovery, and eventual resolution.

Tests have boundaries

A bounded simulation can find counterexamples within the scenarios and state space it explores. It cannot automatically establish safety under all possible concurrency, crashes, malicious inputs, or production conditions. Claims should state their assumptions and limits.

One distinction matters throughout A property can be specified without being enforced. A mechanism can be implemented without being sufficient. A test can pass without proving universal safety. Good governance makes these differences visible.

12. The central idea: govern what must remain true

Governance and middleware are not rivals. Governance defines what must be true; middleware and runtime components help enforce those conditions; evidence and reconciliation help establish whether they remain true.

The deeper shift is to stop treating software layers as the primary definition of governance. Instead, begin with the things that matter: agents, actions, transitions, histories, delegations, shared resources, and system-wide commitments.

Then identify the properties that apply to those things, the relationships that connect them, the moments when the properties must be checked, and the mechanisms and evidence needed to preserve them.

The most useful question is not only “Which layer checks this?” It is: “What must remain true, for which objects and relationships, throughout autonomous execution—and how will we know?”

This perspective connects policy to execution, execution to evidence, and individual actions to the wider outcomes the system is supposed to protect.

It also offers a practical direction for future work: build systems that do not merely authorize agents or block duplicate requests, but preserve meaningful safety properties across interacting objects—even when execution is uncertain, information is incomplete, and no single component sees the whole picture.
